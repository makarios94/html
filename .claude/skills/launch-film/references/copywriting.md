## 1. Ask before you write: the copy questions

Copy written before you know who it is for, what it must achieve and how long it has is a guess. At the start of every
new asset (a film, a cutdown with a new job, an end card with a new offer), get these four answers. Fill each from what
the user already said and the brand sources; ask only what is still open, in one round.

| Question | Why it changes the copy | Offer as the recommended answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Who is it for?** Role, situation, where they see it, and how aware they are (below) | The pain you name, the words you use, how much you must explain | The product's main user, on a phone, in feed; problem-aware |
| **What does success look like?** The one action, and how we will know (a sign-up, a WhatsApp message, a booked demo, a visit, being remembered) | The call to action, the offer, how hard the close pushes, what the end card holds | The brand's main action at its real destination |
| **How long, where, and how many versions?** Length per placement, the aspects that follow, cutdowns, hook variants to test | The word budget, how many stages fit, how many hooks to write | 30 s voice-led or 15 s music-led for Reels and TikTok, a 6 s cut, three hooks |
| **Which framework?** | The order of the argument | Your pick from section 3, with one alternative and one line on when it would win |

**How to ask:** as the skill's question card (SKILL.md, "Asking the user"). These four questions are gate 1,
sent before the concept, each with your recommended answer first. Another decision the next step needs (a claim you
cannot source, how the brand says its name, a spend) joins the same card while it stays at four questions; the rest
wait for gate 2. The same section says when not to ask.

### How aware is the audience?

Eugene Schwartz's five stages of awareness (Breakthrough Advertising, 1966) decide where the copy starts:

| Stage | They know | The copy opens on | Shape (Paidly) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Nothing of the problem as a problem | A moment they recognise | "You did the work weeks ago." |
| Problem-aware | The pain, not that it can be solved | The pain, named in their words | "Chasing payments is a second job." |
| Solution-aware | That fixes exist (reminders by hand, a spreadsheet, a collector) | How this way differs from the one they use | "Add an invoice once. Paidly follows up." |
| Product-aware | That the product exists | The proof and the offer | A real number or customer, then the offer (only what is true) |
| Most aware | They are ready | The action | "Try it free at paidly.app." |

Cold audiences (most paid social) sit in the first two stages: lead with the situation and the pain. Retargeting sits
in the last two: lead with proof and the offer.

## 2. Find the pain

The strongest ads name one pain so precisely that the viewer feels caught.

- **Where pains come from, best first:** the user's own words about their customers; sales calls and support
  messages; reviews of the product and of the alternatives; community threads and comments in the market; the
  product's own onboarding questions. Keep the customer's exact phrases: copy that sounds like the customer outsells
  copy that sounds like the brand.
- **Pick one.** Rank the pains by how often they happen and what they cost (time, money, standing, peace of mind).
  One asset, one pain. A second pain is a second ad.
- **Make it a scene.** A pain lands as a concrete moment with a number or a name the viewer recognises: "You've sent
  the invoice three times, and every reply says 'next week.'" lands where "Late payments are frustrating." does not.
- **Agitate with the cost, honestly.** What the pain takes from them (evenings, cash flow, a client relationship), told
  as their situation. A scenario framed as "you" is fine; a market number needs a source (creative-direction section
  3). No invented statistics, no fear.
- **The turn names it.** One short plain line that reframes the pain ("Chasing payments is a second job."), then the
  product.
- **The solution is verbs.** What the product does for them, one verb per beat, shown on screen ("follows up",
  "tells you"), each tied back to the pain.

## 3. Choose the framework

Recommend from the audience's awareness, the asset's job and the time it has:

| Audience and job | Length | Recommend | Alternative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cold, problem-aware, convert | 25 to 45 s, voice-led | **PAS** | BAB when the change is visible |
| Cold, unaware, be noticed | 15 to 30 s | **Hook, story, offer** | BAB, or a day in the life |
| Solution-aware, using a workaround | 15 to 45 s | **Old way, new way** | FAB demo |
| Product-aware, retargeting | 6 to 15 s | **4Ps** (promise, picture, proof, push) | Objection flip |
| Most aware | 6 s | **One beat** (hook, promise, action) | |
| Music-led, several features, be noticed | 15 s | **AIDA** as a feature parade | Three reasons |
| Brand or founder film, trust | 45 to 90 s | **StoryBrand, short** | A founder's story |
| One new feature | 8 to 12 s | **FAB demo** | One feature, one moment |

Give the recommendation in one line with its reason ("PAS: your buyers already feel the chasing, so naming it stops
them"), one alternative with when it would win, and let the user choose.

## 4. The frameworks, stage by stage

What each stage carries in a voice-led film (VO lines) and in a music-led ad (cards of six words or fewer).

### PAS: problem, agitate, solve

For problem-aware audiences. The spine of the Seeran ad and the Paidly example.

| Stage | Carries | Paidly, voice-led | As cards |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem | The pain as a scene, in their words | "You did the work weeks ago. You've sent the invoice three times." | "Still unpaid?" |
| Agitate | What it costs them, made concrete | "Some clients stop replying at all." | "3 reminders. 0 replies." |
| Turn | The pain named in one plain line | "Chasing payments is a second job." | "Chasing is a second job." |
| Solve | The product doing the work, one verb per beat | "Meet Paidly. Add an invoice once. Paidly follows up on schedule." | "Paidly follows up." |
| Action | One verb, one destination | "Try it free at paidly.app." | "paidly.app" |

### BAB: before, after, bridge

For a change people can see. **Before**: their world now. **After**: their world with it, the payoff. **Bridge**: the
product is how they get there. Then the action. In a 15 s ad the bridge often plays before the after lands, as in
Basket: "Out of groceries?" (before), "Get it all in one app." (bridge), "Shop. Track. Done." (after), logo and "Get
the app".

### Old way, new way: a fair comparison

For audiences already solving the problem another way. **The old way** they use, shown factually (a montage, a little
tedious); **the cut**; **the new way** doing the same job, shown at the speed it really runs; **the difference** in one
plain line; the action. As cards: "Reminder 1. Reminder 2. Reminder 3." over the old way, then "Paidly sends them on
schedule." over the new.

- Compare against the way people do it today (spreadsheets, WhatsApp threads, reminders by hand), shown fairly.
- A named competitor only with current, verifiable facts the user supplies: comparative advertising is regulated in
  most markets.
- Give each side its own plain line. The "it's not X, it's Y" shape stays banned, even here.

### AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action

The general-purpose order; in a music-led ad it becomes a feature parade on the bar. **Attention**: the hook on frame
0. **Interest**: the promise. **Desire**: the features as benefits, one per bar, the product doing each. **Action**:
the logo and the call to action on the last chord. Basket: "Out of groceries?" / "Get it all in one app." / categories,
basket, tracking on the bars / "Shop. Track. Done." / logo, "Get the app".

### FAB demo: feature, advantage, benefit

For a feature drop. **Feature**: what it is, shown. **Advantage**: what it does that the old way did not. **Benefit**:
what the viewer gets (time, money, calm). Run the "so what?" test on every feature line: ask "so what?" until the
answer is something the viewer feels, and write that.

### 4Ps: promise, picture, proof, push

For product-aware audiences, usually retargeting. **Promise**: the outcome, plainly. **Picture**: them with it, one
moment. **Proof**: a real number, a real customer's words, the product doing it live. **Push**: the offer and the
action, with a reason to act now only when the reason is true.

### Hook, story, offer

For cold audiences in short-form feeds. **Hook**: frame 0, a moment they recognise. **Story**: a tiny before and after
with one character, ideally them. **Offer**: what they get, and the action.

### StoryBrand, short

For brand and founder films, after Donald Miller's StoryBrand. The customer is the hero with a problem; the brand is
the guide (empathy and know-how, shown, never claimed); the guide gives a plan of two or three steps and calls them to
act; say what is at stake if nothing changes and what success looks like. The brand never plays the hero.

### One beat

For 6 s and the most aware: a hook that is also the promise, the product in one frame, the action. "Every reminder,
sent for you. paidly.app"

## 5. Prove it without saying "best"

The user will often want the viewer to leave sure that this is the right choice. Copy that says "best", "#1" or "the
only" fails twice: it breaks the truth inventory unless it is sourced, and viewers discount it on sight. Show it
instead:

- **The product doing the job** on screen, at the speed it really runs.
- **Specifics** from the truth inventory: a real number, a real customer's words (with permission), an integration
  that exists.
- **A fair before and after:** the same job done the old way and the new way.
- **The top objection answered** in one line (price, the effort to switch, trust), only with what is true.
- **Risk removed:** a free trial, a guarantee, "no card needed", only when the offer exists.

Superlatives and comparisons with competitors need a source in the truth inventory. Without one, they do not appear.

## 6. The copy deck

What the user sees before the build (template: `assets/templates/copy-brief.md`):

1. The brief in five lines: the audience and how aware they are, the one pain, success and the action, the lengths and
   versions, the framework and why.
2. The framework map: stage | line (VO or card) | what the viewer sees | words and seconds.
3. The full script (`script.txt` for a voice-led film) or the card list (`copy.md` for a music-led ad), inside the word
   budget.
4. Three hook variants (frame 0 and the first line) and two call-to-action variants, for testing.
5. Cutdowns when asked (15 s, 6 s), each keeping the one pain and the action.
6. Post copy in the same framework, compressed: the hook line, the pain, the product, the action.

Get a yes on the copy before the build when the user is there to answer. Otherwise build on your recommendation and
say so.

## 7. Checks before the user sees it

- One message: someone who saw it once can say who it is for and what it does.
- One pain, named in the audience's words; the solution shown as verbs.
- Every claim on the truth inventory; no "best", "#1" or "only" without a source; no invented numbers.
- Hooks pass the four Us (useful, urgent, unique, ultra-specific) and read in under a second.
- House rules: no em dashes, no "it's not X, it's Y", plain declarative sentences, no self-praise, every name spelled
  the owner's way.
- Inside the budget: a VO at 2.3 to 2.9 words per second, read aloud with a timer; cards of six words or fewer, held
  long enough to read twice.
- The audience's register: write how they talk (Nigerian English for a Nigerian audience, with local currency, places
  and names), checked by someone from the market when it matters. Never a caricature.
